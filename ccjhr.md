# Comparative Crime Justice and Human Rights


## Course Objectives

- To examine how human rights operate as constitutional, moral, and institutional restraints on the state’s power to suspect, search, arrest, detain, prosecute, punish, imprison, surveil, and stigmatize.
- To study the criminal justice system through a comparative and institutional lens, with attention to the role of police, prosecution, courts, prisons, legal aid institutions, human rights bodies, media, civil society, and emerging technological actors.
- To analyse procedural justice as a rights-constrained method of making coercive legal decisions under uncertainty, especially in relation to arrest, remand, bail, undertrial detention, fair trial, evidence, victim protection, and punishment.

## Learning Outcomes

- Explain the relationship between social contract theory, state coercion, criminal justice, and human rights.
- Critically analyse the legitimacy of state action at different stages of the criminal process, including search, arrest, custody, bail, trial, imprisonment, and punishment.
- Apply constitutional, statutory, comparative, and international human rights standards to issues involving accused persons, victims, prisoners, witnesses, and other stakeholders in the criminal justice system.
- Evaluate criminal justice decisions through the lenses of legality, dignity, necessity, proportionality, due process, accountability, and review.
- Use basic probabilistic reasoning to assess how courts and institutions make decisions under uncertainty, especially in relation to risk, bail, detention, evidentiary thresholds, and procedural error.
- Compare Indian criminal justice institutions with selected comparative models and identify how institutional design affects the protection or erosion of human rights.
- Critique contemporary developments such as media trials, surveillance, predictive policing, algorithmic risk tools, and digital vigilantism from a human rights perspective.
- Design reasoned legal responses, policy protocols, reform proposals, or decision records that balance liberty, public safety, victim protection, fair trial, and institutional legitimacy.

## Module 1 - Before Crime: Social Contract, Human Rights, and the Coercive State (6 hours)

### Key questions

- What is the private sphere, and why does it matter before we speak of crime?
- Why do individuals surrender private revenge to public authority?
- The concept of crime, and the historical development of criminal liability (movement from vengeance to public prosecution to liability to prosecution)
- What does the state gain through the social contract, and what does the individual never surrender?
- What makes state coercion legitimate rather than merely powerful?
- When may the state enter the body, home, movement, reputation, and liberty of a person?
- How should courts make coercive decisions under uncertainty?

### Theoretical anchors

- Social contract: Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Rawls.
- Private sphere and liberty: Mill, Raz, Puttaswamy.
- Human dignity and anti-instrumentalism: Kant, Dworkin, constitutional morality.
- Monopoly over violence: Weber.
- Due process vs crime control: Packer.
- Probability and risk: Marletta 2025; Bayesian updating; Type I/Type II errors.

### Thought experiments / provocations

- Hobbesian state of nature: why escape private vengeance?
- Island compact: what coercive powers would we design from scratch?
- Rawlsian veil of ignorance: what rules would we choose before knowing whether we are victim, accused, police, judge, or prisoner?
- Glass house citizen: can total surveillance be justified by safety?
- Benevolent dictator-police: can effectiveness substitute legality?
- Ticking bomb: can emergency suspend dignity?
- Police and innocent man: may one innocent person be sacrificed for public order?
- Risk machine: can probability justify detention?

### Legal and institutional anchors

- Constitution of India: Articles 14, 19, 20, 21, 22, 32, 39A, 226.
- Current criminal law framework: Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam.
- International anchors: UDHR, ICCPR, Convention Against Torture, ECHR, Nelson Mandela Rules.
- Core Indian cases to introduce: Maneka Gandhi, D.K. Basu, Joginder Kumar, Arnesh Kumar, Puttaswamy, Hussainara Khatoon, Khatri, Sunil Batra.

### Recommended sources

- Hobbes, Leviathan.
- Locke, Second Treatise of Government.
- Rousseau, The Social Contract.
- Rawls, A Theory of Justice.
- Weber, Politics as a Vocation.
- Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals.
- Dworkin, Taking Rights Seriously.
- Packer, The Limits of the Criminal Sanction.
- Marletta, Probabilities, costs, and justice, Law, Probability and Risk, 2025.
- Puttaswamy; D.K. Basu; Arnesh Kumar; Hussainara Khatoon; Sunil Batra.

## Module 2 - Comparative Criminal Justice Institutions (4 hours)

- **Position in the course flow:** Moves from philosophical legitimacy to institutional machinery. Students compare how different systems organize police, prosecution, courts, defence, legal aid, and prisons.

### Key questions

- Do criminal justice systems differ only in procedure, or in their image of the human person?
- What is the difference between adversarial, inquisitorial, and hybrid systems?
- How does institutional design affect human rights protection?
- Which institution is most responsible for preventing rights collapse: police, prosecutor, magistrate, trial court, prison authority, or legal aid?

### Theoretical anchors

- Comparative criminal process (inquisitorial v. adversarial).
- Components of criminal justice system and role in justice delivery and HR protection
- Packer: crime control vs due process.
- Institutionalism: incentives, discretion, path dependence, workload, hierarchy, and accountability.
- Comparative study with US, role of police, prosecution, and courts
- Systems thinking: rights violations as institutional outputs, not merely individual misconduct.

### Thought experiments / provocations

- Rawlsian role lottery: assign students roles in different systems and ask which procedural safeguards they would demand.
- Benevolent dictator-police: test whether institutional outcomes can be judged by crime reduction alone.

### Legal and institutional anchors

- India: police, prosecution, courts, legal aid, prisons, human rights commissions.
- US comparison: probable cause, Miranda, Gideon, plea bargaining, bail and pretrial detention debates.
- UK/ECHR comparison: fair trial, counsel, detention safeguards, rights against ill-treatment.
- Global South lens: overcrowding, undertrial detention, access to counsel, police violence, informal justice.

### Recommended sources

- Mirjan Damaska, The Faces of Justice and State Authority.
- Herbert Packer, The Limits of the Criminal Sanction.
- Mireille Delmas-Marty and comparative criminal process materials.
- Upendra Baxi, The Crisis of the Indian Legal System.
- UNODC and international human rights criminal justice materials.
- Indian statutory and constitutional materials on police, prosecution, courts, and legal aid.

## Module 3 - Policing, Arrest, Custody, and Interrogation (5 hours)

- **Position in the course flow:** Examines the first and most dangerous point of contact between person and state. This module operationalizes the private-sphere framework from Module 1.

### Key questions

- When does suspicion become coercion?
- What does arrest do to autonomy, reputation, movement, livelihood, and family life?
- Why are rights weakest when the person most needs them?
- Can custodial interrogation be rights-compatible?
- What procedural safeguards convert police power into legally reviewable power?

### Theoretical anchors

- Weber: police as frontline monopoly over violence.
- Kant: suspect is not merely an instrument of investigation.
- Foucault: investigation, surveillance, and discipline.
- Information asymmetry: police case diary, accused vulnerability, magistrate dependence on state information.
- Procedural justice: documentation, reasons, counsel, medical examination, judicial review.

### Thought experiments / provocations

- Ring of Gyges: invisible police officer and the need for institutional accountability.
- Ticking bomb: torture and the danger of emergency exceptions.
- Glass house citizen: surveillance and the collapse of privacy.

### Legal and institutional anchors

- Arrest powers, search powers, remand, legal aid, confession, right against self-incrimination, magistrate oversight.
- Indian cases: D.K. Basu; Joginder Kumar; Arnesh Kumar; Selvi; Nandini Satpathy; Khatri; Sheela Barse.
- Comparative materials: Miranda v Arizona; Terry v Ohio; Salduz v Turkey; Gafgen v Germany.
- Human rights instruments: ICCPR Articles 7, 9, 10, 14; Convention Against Torture; UN Body of Principles for Detention.

### Recommended sources

- D.K. Basu v State of West Bengal.
- Joginder Kumar v State of U.P.
- Arnesh Kumar v State of Bihar.
- Selvi v State of Karnataka.
- Nandini Satpathy v P.L. Dani.
- Miranda v Arizona.
- Salduz v Turkey.
- Gafgen v Germany.
- NHRC materials on custodial violence.

## Module 4 - Bail, Remand, Undertrial Detention, and Procedural Uncertainty (5 hours)

- **Position in the course flow:** Places the probabilistic approach at the centre of the course. This module treats pretrial liberty as the clearest site where courts predict risk under incomplete information.

### Key questions

- When does process itself become punishment?
- How much uncertainty can justify how much coercion?
- What is the moral difference between detaining the guilty after trial and detaining the accused before trial?
- How should courts compare risk of flight, risk of tampering, victim safety, public order, and liberty?
- Are bail decisions doctrinal decisions, probabilistic decisions, or moral decisions?

### Theoretical anchors

- Presumption of innocence.
- Marletta: precautionary measures as probabilistic decisions under uncertainty.
- Type I error: wrongful detention or excessive restriction.
- Type II error: wrongful release where serious preventable harm occurs.
- Bayesian updating: courts revise beliefs as more evidence emerges.
- Rights-constrained cost-benefit: probability informs, rights constrain.

### Thought experiments / provocations

- Risk machine: algorithm predicts future flight/reoffending.
- Criminal justice trolley: preventive detention of one person to reduce collective risk.
- Police and innocent man: public order cannot justify punishing the innocent.

### Legal and institutional anchors

- Bail, remand, surety, undertrial detention, legal aid, speedy trial, periodic review.
- Indian cases: Gudikanti Narasimhulu; Babu Singh; Moti Ram; Sanjay Chandra; Siddharam Satlingappa Mhetre; Satender Kumar Antil; K.A. Najeeb; Hussainara Khatoon.
- Comparative materials: United States v Salerno; pretrial risk assessment debates; ECHR Article 5 detention jurisprudence.
- Institutional materials: prison statistics, undertrial data, legal aid reports, bail reform reports.

### Recommended sources

- Massimiliano Marletta, Probabilities, costs, and justice, Law, Probability and Risk, 2025.
- Richard J. Allen, Burdens of Proof.
- Michael O. Finkelstein, Basic Concepts of Probability and Statistics in the Law.
- Gudikanti Narasimhulu v Public Prosecutor.
- Babu Singh v State of U.P.
- Moti Ram v State of M.P.
- Sanjay Chandra v CBI.
- Satender Kumar Antil v CBI.
- K.A. Najeeb.
- Hussainara Khatoon line of cases.

## Module 5 - Fair Trial, Evidence, Victims, and Witnesses (4 hours)

- **Position in the course flow:** Moves from pretrial coercion to adjudicative fairness. The module refuses a simplistic accused-rights versus victim-rights binary and frames trial design as a rights-balancing institutional problem.

### Key questions

- What makes a criminal trial fair?
- Fair to whom: accused, victim, witness, community, or constitutional order?
- Can victim participation coexist with presumption of innocence?
- How should law protect vulnerable witnesses without weakening due process?
- How should electronic evidence and forensic science alter procedural safeguards?

### Theoretical anchors

- Due process and adversarial fairness.
- Epistemic justice: how legal systems produce, test, exclude, and trust evidence.
- Victimology and secondary victimization.
- Capability approach: ability of victim and accused to meaningfully participate.
- Probability and proof: standards of proof, evidentiary inference, and reasonable doubt.

### Thought experiments / provocations

- Rawlsian role lottery: design a trial without knowing whether one is victim, accused, witness, or judge.
- Grudge informer: legality, morality, and criminal evidence in unjust regimes.
- Lifeboat: necessity, moral luck, and tragic responsibility.

### Legal and institutional anchors

- Fair trial rights, open courts, legal representation, cross-examination, witness protection, victim participation, vulnerable witness procedures, electronic evidence, forensic proof.
- Indian cases: Zahira Habibullah Sheikh; State of Maharashtra v Dr. Praful B. Desai; Anvar P.V.; Arjun Panditrao; Sakshi v Union of India; Nipun Saxena.
- Comparative materials: Gideon v Wainwright; Crawford v Washington; ECHR Article 6 jurisprudence.
- International anchors: ICCPR Article 14; UN Declaration of Basic Principles of Justice for Victims of Crime and Abuse of Power.

### Recommended sources

- ICCPR Article 14.
- UN Victims Declaration, 1985.
- Andrew Ashworth and Mike Redmayne, The Criminal Process.
- Mirjan Damaska, Evidence Law Adrift.
- Zahira Habibullah Sheikh v State of Gujarat.
- Sakshi v Union of India.
- Anvar P.V. v P.K. Basheer.
- Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal.

## Module 6 - Punishment, Prison, Release, and Social Death (4 hours)

- **Position in the course flow:** Tests whether human rights survive conviction. The module shifts attention from courtroom justice to carceral justice, dignity, rehabilitation, and post-release stigma.

### Key questions

- Do human rights end after conviction?
- What exactly does imprisonment lawfully take away: liberty only, or dignity too?
- Can prison be compatible with autonomy, health, family life, education, and rehabilitation?
- Is punishment justified as retribution, deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation, restoration, or expressive condemnation?
- What forms of punishment exist after formal sentence: stigma, exclusion, unemployment, surveillance, and social death?

### Theoretical anchors

- Theories of punishment: retributive, utilitarian, rehabilitative, restorative, communicative.
- Foucault: prison as disciplinary power.
- Garland: penal culture and governance of risk.
- Dignity after conviction: limited deprivation, not civil death.
- Abolitionist and decarceral critiques.

### Thought experiments / provocations

- Panopticon: surveillance and discipline in prison and society.
- Experience machine: safe society without freedom.
- Lifeboat: extreme necessity, moral tragedy, and proportional punishment.

### Legal and institutional anchors

- Prisoners' rights, overcrowding, solitary confinement, prison labour, health, mental health, women prisoners, transgender prisoners, parole, remission, open prisons, death penalty, mercy jurisdiction.
- Indian cases: Sunil Batra; Charles Sobraj; Prem Shankar Shukla; Francis Coralie Mullin; Sheela Barse; Bachan Singh; Machhi Singh; Shatrughan Chauhan; Mohd Arif.
- International anchors: Nelson Mandela Rules, Bangkok Rules, Tokyo Rules, ICCPR Articles 6, 7, 10.

### Recommended sources

- Michel Foucault, Discipline and Punish.
- David Garland, Punishment and Modern Society; The Culture of Control.
- Antony Duff, Punishment, Communication, and Community.
- H.L.A. Hart, Punishment and Responsibility.
- Angela Y. Davis, Are Prisons Obsolete?
- UN Nelson Mandela Rules.
- Sunil Batra v Delhi Administration.
- Francis Coralie Mullin v Administrator, Union Territory of Delhi.
- Bachan Singh v State of Punjab.
- Shatrughan Chauhan v Union of India.

## Module 7 - Media, Civil Society, Technology, and Criminal Justice Futures (2 hours)

- **Position in the course flow:** Concludes by asking whether contemporary criminal justice is becoming more humane or merely more efficient at coercion.

### Key questions

- Can media speed up justice without destroying due process?
- When does public outrage become informal punishment?
- What is the role of NGOs, human rights commissions, legal aid groups, and prison reform movements?
- Do surveillance technologies, predictive policing, facial recognition, and algorithmic risk tools deepen justice or intensify suspicion?
- What would a rights-respecting criminal justice system look like in the next decade?

### Theoretical anchors

- Foucault and panoptic visibility.
- Nozick and the distinction between felt security and real freedom.
- Algorithmic governance and procedural due process.
- Civil society as counter-power.
- Restorative and decarceral imagination.

### Thought experiments / provocations

- Glass house citizen: safety through total visibility.
- Risk machine: algorithmic bail and risk assessment.
- Experience machine: citizens feel safe while being invisibly governed.

### Legal and institutional anchors

- Media trial, contempt, reputation, presumption of innocence, victim privacy, digital vigilantism, legal aid, NGOs, human rights commissions, technology in criminal process.
- Indian anchors: Sahara India Real Estate; Manu Sharma; Nipun Saxena; privacy and surveillance jurisprudence; human rights commission materials.
- Comparative anchors: algorithmic risk assessment debates; facial recognition and policing reports; ECHR privacy jurisprudence.

### Recommended sources

- Foucault, Discipline and Punish.
- Bentham, Panopticon.
- Nozick, Anarchy, State, and Utopia.
- Sharad Goel et al., The Accuracy, Equity, and Jurisprudence of Criminal Risk Assessment.
- Sahara India Real Estate Corp. v SEBI.
- Nipun Saxena v Union of India.
- NHRC and civil society reports on prisons, custody, legal aid, and human rights.
